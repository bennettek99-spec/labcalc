The formulas behind the calculators

Addition & subtraction

δQ = √( δa² + δb² )

Absolute uncertainties combine in quadrature. Works for Q = a + b and Q = a − b alike — uncertainty never shrinks when you subtract.

Multiplication & division

δQ/|Q| = √( (δa/a)² + (δb/b)² )

Relative (fractional) uncertainties combine in quadrature for products and quotients.

Powers

δQ/|Q| = |n| · δa/|a|

For Q = aⁿ with an exact exponent, the relative uncertainty scales by |n|. Squaring a value doubles its relative uncertainty.

Standard error of the mean

SEM = s / √n

s is the sample standard deviation. The more trials you run, the tighter the uncertainty on your mean — that's why repeating measurements matters.

FAQ

Why add uncertainties in quadrature instead of just adding them?

If the errors in a and b are independent and random, it's unlikely both are at their maximum at the same time — they partially cancel. Adding in quadrature (square, sum, square-root) accounts for that cancellation and gives the statistically expected uncertainty. Simply adding δa + δb overestimates it.

Are trailing zeros significant?

After a decimal point, yes: 1.200 has four sig figs. In a whole number with no decimal point, trailing zeros are ambiguous and conventionally treated as not significant: 1200 has two. To be unambiguous, write 1.200 × 10³. This calculator follows that convention.

Should I report standard deviation or standard error?

Both have a job. The standard deviation (s) describes the spread of your individual trials. The standard error (SEM = s/√n) is the uncertainty on your mean. When you quote a final result, write it as mean ± SEM.

How many sig figs should my uncertainty have?

Round the uncertainty to one significant figure (two if its leading digit is 1), then round your value to the same decimal place. So 9.81473 ± 0.02835 becomes 9.81 ± 0.03. The error propagation calculator above does this formatting for you.

Can I cite these methods in my lab report?

The formulas are the standard ones — cite J. R. Taylor, An Introduction to Error Analysis, 2nd ed. (University Science Books, 1997), which is where every intro lab manual gets them.
