Standard Deviation vs. Standard Error of the Mean

They look similar and students mix them up constantly, but they answer different questions. Standard deviation tells you how spread out your individual measurements are. Standard error tells you how precisely you've pinned down their average. Knowing which is which is the difference between a result that reads correctly and one that overstates your precision.

Standard deviation: the spread of your data

The sample standard deviation (s) measures how far individual measurements typically fall from their mean. A small s means your trials clustered tightly; a large s means they scattered.

Sample standard deviations = √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) )

The divisor is n − 1, not n — this is Bessel's correction, which keeps the sample standard deviation from systematically underestimating the true spread of the population.

Standard error: the uncertainty in your mean

The standard error of the mean (SEM) measures how much your calculated mean would wobble if you repeated the whole experiment. It's the standard deviation scaled down by the number of measurements:

Standard error of the meanSEM = s / √n

Because you divide by √n, the SEM is always smaller than the standard deviation, and it shrinks as you take more data.

The key difference, in one line

Standard deviation describes your data; standard error describes your estimate of the mean. Take ten times as many measurements and the standard deviation barely changes (it's converging on a real property of what you're measuring), but the standard error drops by a factor of √10 ≈ 3.2 — averaging more trials pins down the mean more tightly. That's the statistical reason repeating measurements is worth the effort.

Worked example

Six measurements of g (m/s²): 9.79, 9.83, 9.81, 9.78, 9.85, 9.80.

Mean x̄ = 9.8100  ·  s = 0.0261  ·  n = 6, so √n = 2.449
SEM = 0.0261 / 2.449 = 0.0107
Report as: g = 9.810 ± 0.011 m/s² (mean ± SEM, uncertainty to two sig figs since it leads with 1).

Which one goes in your lab report?

Both have a place, and a strong report uses them deliberately:

  • Use the standard deviation when you're describing the spread of your trials ("the measurements had a standard deviation of 0.03 m/s²").
  • Use the standard error as the uncertainty on your final answer. Quote the result as mean ± SEM.

To compare that result against an accepted value, see whether the accepted value falls within about two standard errors of your mean — that's covered in the error propagation guide.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between standard deviation and standard error?

Standard deviation describes the spread of your individual measurements; standard error describes the uncertainty in their mean. SEM = s/√n, so the standard error is always the smaller of the two.

Should I report standard deviation or standard error?

Report the standard deviation to describe the spread of your trials, and the standard error as the uncertainty on your best estimate. The final result is usually written mean ± SEM.

Why divide by n − 1 instead of n?

Using n − 1 (Bessel's correction) prevents the sample standard deviation from systematically underestimating the true population spread, because the sample mean is itself estimated from the same data.
