How to Propagate Uncertainty (Error Propagation)

Every measurement carries an uncertainty, and when you combine measurements in a calculation, those uncertainties carry through to your result. Error propagation is the set of rules for finding the uncertainty in a calculated quantity from the uncertainties in the values you measured. Here are the rules every intro physics and chemistry lab uses, with worked examples.

Why uncertainties combine "in quadrature"

When the errors in your measurements are independent and random, it's unlikely that every measurement is off in the same direction by its maximum amount at the same time. The errors partially cancel. Combining them in quadrature — square each one, add, then take the square root — captures that cancellation and gives the statistically expected uncertainty.

Simply adding the uncertainties (δa + δb) assumes the worst case, where every error lines up against you, and overestimates the result. Use direct addition only as a deliberately conservative bound, or when the errors are fully correlated rather than independent.

Rule 1 — Addition and subtraction

For Q = a + b or Q = a − b, the absolute uncertainties combine in quadrature:

Add / SubtractδQ = √( δa² + δb² )

Notice the uncertainty is identical whether you add or subtract — subtraction never shrinks it. In fact, subtracting two nearly equal numbers produces a small result with a large relative uncertainty. This "subtractive cancellation" is a classic trap in lab work.

Example: (12.4 ± 0.2) + (3.1 ± 0.1).   δQ = √(0.2² + 0.1²) = √0.05 = 0.22 → Q = 15.5 ± 0.2.

Rule 2 — Multiplication and division

For Q = a × b or Q = a ÷ b, the relative (fractional) uncertainties combine in quadrature:

Multiply / DivideδQ / |Q| = √( (δa/a)² + (δb/b)² )
Example: (12.4 ± 0.2) × (3.1 ± 0.1).   relative = √((0.2/12.4)² + (0.1/3.1)²) = √(0.0161² + 0.0323²) = 3.6%.   Q = 38.44, so δQ = 0.036 × 38.44 = 1.4 → Q = 38.4 ± 1.4.

Rule 3 — Powers

For Q = aⁿ with an exact exponent n, the relative uncertainty scales by |n|:

PowersδQ / |Q| = |n| · δa / |a|

Squaring a value doubles its relative uncertainty; taking a square root halves it. This is why a quantity that depends on the square of a measurement (like kinetic energy on velocity) is so sensitive to that measurement's precision.

Example: (3.1 ± 0.1)².   relative = 2 × (0.1/3.1) = 6.5%.   Q = 9.61, δQ = 0.6 → Q = 9.6 ± 0.6.

Rule 4 — Multiplying by an exact constant

For Q = c · a where c is an exact number (like 2 or π), the uncertainty scales directly and the relative uncertainty is unchanged:

Constant multipleδQ = |c| · δa

The general formula behind all of these

Every rule above is a special case of the general error-propagation formula. For any function Q = f(a, b, …) of independent variables:

General caseδQ = √( (∂f/∂a · δa)² + (∂f/∂b · δb)² + … )

You take the partial derivative of your function with respect to each measured variable, multiply by that variable's uncertainty, and add the terms in quadrature. The add, multiply, and power rules are just this formula worked out for the common operations — handy to know when your formula doesn't fit a simple template.

How to round and report your result

Once you have δQ, format it the way lab reports expect:

  1. Round the uncertainty to one significant figure — or two if its leading digit is 1.
  2. Round the value to the same decimal place as the uncertainty.
  3. Write both with shared units, e.g. (9.79 ± 0.04) m/s².

So a raw result of 9.81473 ± 0.02835 is reported as 9.81 ± 0.03. For more on rounding, see the significant figures guide.

Frequently asked questions

Do I add uncertainties or combine them in quadrature?

For independent, random errors, combine them in quadrature — the errors partially cancel, so direct addition overestimates the uncertainty. Add directly only as a conservative upper bound or when the errors are fully correlated.

Does subtracting two measurements reduce the uncertainty?

No. For Q = a − b the uncertainty equals that of Q = a + b. And subtracting two nearly equal numbers gives a small result with a large relative uncertainty (subtractive cancellation), which can wreck precision.

What about systematic errors?

These rules apply to independent random errors. Systematic errors (a miscalibrated instrument, a consistent reading bias) don't cancel and are handled separately — identify and correct them, or report them as a separate contribution.

Where do these formulas come from?

They're the standard rules from J. R. Taylor, An Introduction to Error Analysis, 2nd ed. (University Science Books, 1997) — the reference most intro lab manuals draw on. It's the right citation for your methods section.
