Significant Figures Rules, Explained

Significant figures are the digits in a measurement that carry real information about its precision. Get them right and your results communicate exactly how well you measured; get them wrong and you either throw away precision or claim more than your instrument can deliver. Here are the rules, with examples.

What counts as a significant figure

Read a number left to right and apply these in order:

  • All non-zero digits are significant. 47.3 has three.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits ("captive" zeros) are significant. 4007 has four.
  • Leading zeros are never significant — they only set the decimal place. 0.00450 has three (4, 5, 0).
  • Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. 1.200 has four; the zeros say "I measured to the thousandths place."
  • Trailing zeros in a whole number with no decimal point are ambiguous. 1200 could be two, three, or four. By convention they're treated as not significant (so: two), but the only unambiguous fix is scientific notation.

Use scientific notation to be unambiguous

Scientific notation makes the number of significant figures explicit, because every digit you write in the mantissa is significant. If you measured 1200 to the nearest hundred, write 1.2 × 10³ (two sig figs). If you measured it to the nearest unit, write 1.200 × 10³ (four). The reader never has to guess.

Significant figures in calculations

The rule depends on the operation — and it's a different rule for the two families:

Multiplication and division

The result keeps as many significant figures as the input with the fewest.

Example: 4.56 × 1.4 = 6.384 → round to 6.4, because 1.4 has only two sig figs.

Addition and subtraction

The result keeps as many decimal places as the input with the fewest decimal places — count places after the point, not total digits.

Example: 12.11 + 0.3 = 12.41 → round to 12.4, because 0.3 is known only to the tenths place.

Carry extra digits until the end. Round only your final answer, not the intermediate steps — rounding too early introduces "rounding error" that can shift your last reported digit.

Sig figs vs. uncertainty

Significant figures are a quick shorthand for precision, but a stated uncertainty (like ± 0.03) is more precise and is what lab reports usually want. When you have an uncertainty, round it to one significant figure (two if its leading digit is 1), then round the value to match. Frequently asked questions

Are trailing zeros significant?

After a decimal point, yes — 1.200 has four. In a whole number with no decimal point they're ambiguous and conventionally treated as not significant, so 1200 has two. Write 1.200 × 10³ to be explicit.

Are leading zeros significant?

Never. They only mark the decimal place. 0.00450 has three significant figures.

Do exact numbers have significant figures?

Exact values — counted quantities (5 trials) and defined constants (exactly 100 cm per m) — have infinite significant figures and never limit the precision of a calculation.
