How to Calculate Percent Error

Percent error tells you how close your measured result came to the accepted ("true") value, as a percentage. It's the standard way intro labs measure accuracy — and it's a one-line calculation once you know the formula. Here's how it works, with a worked example and the distinctions students most often get wrong.

The percent error formula

Percent errorpercent error = |measured − accepted| / |accepted| × 100%

In words: take the difference between your measured value and the accepted value, drop the sign (absolute value), divide by the accepted value, and multiply by 100. The accepted value goes in the denominator because it's the reference you're judging your measurement against.

Step by step

  1. Subtract the accepted value from your measured value.
  2. Take the absolute value of that difference (percent error is reported as a positive number).
  3. Divide by the accepted value.
  4. Multiply by 100 and add a % sign.
Example: you measure gravitational acceleration as 9.79 m/s²; the accepted value is 9.81 m/s².
|9.79 − 9.81| = 0.02  →  0.02 / 9.81 = 0.00204  →  × 100 = 0.20% error.

Should you keep the sign?

Standard percent error uses the absolute value, so it's always positive. But your discussion section often benefits from noting the direction: a signed result of −0.20% tells the reader your measurement came out lower than accepted, which is a clue about systematic error (a consistently slow timer, a miscalibrated scale). Report the positive percent error as your headline number, and mention the direction when you analyze why.

Percent error vs. percent difference

These get swapped constantly. The rule is about whether a "true" value exists:

  • Percent error — you're comparing a measurement to a known accepted value (a textbook constant, a labeled concentration).
  • Percent difference — you're comparing two measured values and neither is the official truth (trial 1 vs. trial 2, your value vs. a lab partner's). Divide by the average of the two:
Percent differencepercent difference = |a − b| / ((a + b) / 2) × 100%

Percent error vs. percent uncertainty

Another pair that's easy to confuse — and the distinction is the heart of a good lab report:

  • Percent error measures accuracy: how close you landed to the accepted value.
  • Percent uncertainty (relative uncertainty) measures precision: how tightly your own measurement is pinned down, regardless of the accepted value. It comes from error propagation.

The insight graders reward: compare the two. If your percent error is smaller than your percent uncertainty, your result is consistent with the accepted value — any difference is explained by measurement uncertainty, not a mistake. If percent error is much larger than your uncertainty, that points to a systematic error worth discussing.

What counts as a "good" percent error?

It depends on the experiment and the equipment, but as a rough guide for intro labs: under ~5% is good, under ~10% is acceptable, and anything larger deserves a real explanation in your discussion. Don't fixate on the number alone, though — a 3% error with 0.5% uncertainty is actually a worse result (an unexplained discrepancy) than a 3% error with 4% uncertainty (fully consistent). Accuracy is only meaningful next to precision.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by the measured value instead of the accepted value. The accepted value is the reference and belongs in the denominator.
  • Forgetting the absolute value and reporting a negative percent error as your headline figure.
  • Using percent error when there's no accepted value — that's a percent difference.
  • Reporting it to too many digits. Two significant figures is almost always enough (0.20%, not 0.2038%).

Frequently asked questions

What is the percent error formula?

Percent error = |measured − accepted| ÷ |accepted| × 100%. Divide the absolute difference between your result and the accepted value by the accepted value, then multiply by 100.

What is a good percent error?

In intro labs, under about 5% is good and under 10% is acceptable — but what matters most is whether the difference is covered by your measurement uncertainty.

Can percent error be over 100%?

Yes. If your measured value is more than double the accepted value, percent error exceeds 100%. It usually signals a unit mistake or a calculation error worth rechecking.
